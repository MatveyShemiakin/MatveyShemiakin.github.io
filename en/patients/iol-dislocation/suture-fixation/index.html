IOL dislocation · surgical treatment

How is scleral fixation of an IOL performed?

What patients often call “suturing an IOL”, when additional fixation is needed, how the operation is performed and what recovery depends on.

“Suturing an IOL” is a patient-friendly term for surgery that gives an intraocular lens additional support when the capsular bag or zonules cannot hold it securely. In practice, scleral fixation may be sutured or sutureless; the technique is selected according to ocular anatomy and the IOL itself.
!
Red flags

When not to wait for a routine appointment

Sudden or rapidly worsening vision, severe pain, marked redness, nausea or vomiting with eye pain, flashes, many new floaters, a “curtain” in the field of vision or eye trauma require urgent ophthalmic assessment.

01

The procedure

IOL fixation in plain language

The aim is to restore a stable central optical system and address risks caused by a mobile or malpositioned intraocular lens.

When is sutured or additional IOL fixation needed?+
Key pointFixation is considered when capsular or zonular support is insufficient and the IOL cannot be held reliably in the correct position without additional support.

This can occur with late dislocation of the “IOL–capsular bag” complex, marked zonular weakness, eye trauma or other situations in which normal capsular support has been lost.

The decision is not based on the amount of displacement alone. The ophthalmologist also assesses IOL stability, visual quality, intraocular pressure and the condition of the cornea, iris, vitreous, retina and optic nerve.

Important

A small stable displacement without significant symptoms or complications may sometimes be observed. The presence of IOL displacement does not automatically mean that surgery is required.

Can the existing IOL always be retained?+
Key pointNo. The existing lens can be retained when its design, condition and position allow safe repositioning and reliable fixation.

If the IOL is undamaged and suitable for the planned fixation method, the surgeon may return it to the correct position and secure it.

If the lens is damaged, unsuitable for reliable fixation or needs to be removed for another reason, IOL exchange is performed and a different lens is implanted.

Important

In complex cases, whether the existing IOL can be retained may only become clear after complete examination or even during surgery.

How is scleral fixation of an IOL performed?+
Key pointThe surgeon gains access to the IOL, frees it from interfering tissue, performs vitrectomy if needed, centres the existing or a new lens and creates a stable new support.

Typical sequence

  1. Preparation and anaesthesia. Local anaesthesia is selected, with sedation when appropriate, according to the procedure and the patient’s general condition.
  2. Assessment of the IOL. The surgeon confirms whether the existing lens can be retained and which ocular structures can provide fixation.
  3. Management of vitreous. An anterior or posterior vitrectomy may be required when vitreous is involved or the IOL has moved posteriorly.
  4. Repositioning or implantation. The existing or a new IOL is placed in the intended central position.
  5. Fixation. The supporting elements are stabilised using the selected method, such as sutured or sutureless scleral fixation.
  6. Final checks. IOL centration and stability, the anterior chamber, wounds and intraocular structures are assessed.

Important

This is a general sequence, not a universal surgical protocol. The exact steps depend on the IOL design, capsular support, vitreous and the condition of the rest of the eye.

Does IOL fixation always use sutures?+
Key pointNo. Both sutured and sutureless scleral fixation techniques are used.

With sutured fixation, the IOL or the “IOL–capsular bag” complex is stabilised using suture material secured in the scleral region. With sutureless techniques, IOL haptics are fixed in scleral tunnels or by another method that does not rely on a permanent supporting suture.

Iris fixation and other forms of secondary IOL implantation are also used in selected situations.

Important

The technique should not be chosen by name alone. Ocular anatomy, IOL design, tissue condition and the surgeon’s experience with the method all matter.

Is vitrectomy always required during IOL fixation?+
Key pointNo. Vitrectomy may be required when vitreous is involved in the area of displacement or when the IOL has moved into the posterior segment.

Removing part of the vitreous can allow the displaced lens to be freed safely, reduce retinal traction and provide access to an IOL located posteriorly.

The extent of vitrectomy is individual. The need for this step does not mean that every patient undergoes the same procedure.

Important

The need for vitrectomy cannot always be determined in advance; in some cases the final decision is made during surgery.

Is the operation painful and how long does it take?+
Key pointMost procedures are performed under local anaesthesia, sometimes with sedation. Significant pain should not be expected during surgery.

Patients may notice touch, water, light or movement. Any significant discomfort should be reported to the surgical team immediately.

Operating time can only be estimated in advance. It depends on IOL position, capsular support, whether vitrectomy is required, whether the original lens can be retained and which fixation method is used.

Important

Same-day care or hospital admission depends on surgical complexity, the patient’s general condition and the organisation’s care pathway.

What is recovery like after IOL fixation?+
Key pointVision may remain blurred or unstable during the first days. Recovery speed depends on the extent of surgery and the pre-existing condition of the eye.

Early visual quality may be affected by corneal oedema, postoperative inflammation, a dilated pupil and adaptation to the changed position or design of the IOL.

Use only prescribed medication, do not rub the eye, follow the restrictions in your discharge instructions and attend scheduled postoperative examinations.

Return to heavy exercise, swimming, driving and final spectacle correction should be individualised.

Important

Increasing pain, worsening redness or deteriorating vision after surgery should not be considered a normal part of recovery.

What complications can occur?+
Key pointRisk depends on the condition of the eye and the technique used and should be discussed individually before surgery.

Possible complications include inflammation or infection, high or low intraocular pressure, haemorrhage, corneal or macular oedema, pupil distortion, residual tilt or recurrent IOL displacement, retinal tear or detachment, and the need for further surgery.

Modern sutured and sutureless scleral fixation techniques can provide good visual outcomes, but no single method is optimal for every eye.

Important

Visual prognosis depends not only on IOL position but also on the condition of the cornea, macula, retina, optic nerve and intraocular pressure.
