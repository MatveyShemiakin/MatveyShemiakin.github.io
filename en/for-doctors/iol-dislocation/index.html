IOL dislocation

A practical decision pathway from defining the position and stability of the IOL–capsular bag complex to selecting observation, repositioning, IOL exchange, secondary fixation and the need for a posterior-segment approach.

Intended for qualified ophthalmologists. In the Russian Federation, current Ministry of Health clinical guidelines, device and drug instructions, local SOPs and individual clinical judgement remain the primary framework.

01 · KEY PRINCIPLES

Four principles that determine management

Principle 1. Treat the clinical problem, not the photograph

A small stable decentration without meaningful symptoms or complications may be followed. Surgery is driven by symptoms, instability and/or complications.

Principle 2. Define what has lost support

Distinguish displacement of the IOL within/relative to the bag from late dislocation of the entire IOL–capsular bag complex.

Principle 3. Assess the vitreous before manipulating the lens

If vitreous has prolapsed anteriorly, traction should be identified and released before active IOL manipulation to reduce retinal traction.

Principle 4. The capsular–zonular apparatus determines the operation

If the IOL–bag complex can be reliably preserved, repositioning and fixation may be appropriate. With inadequate support, unsuitable IOL design or inability to obtain stable centration, removal and IOL exchange with secondary fixation should be considered.

02 · CLASSIFICATION

Describe the anatomy that changes the decision

Timing
  • Early: often related to technical factors, capsular rupture or pre-existing zonular weakness.
  • Late: may occur years later with progressive zonular failure and capsular contraction.
Anatomy
  • In-the-bag IOL–capsular complex dislocation.
  • IOL displacement relative to the capsular bag.
  • Anterior displacement, pupillary capture or posterior dislocation.
03 · RISK FACTORS

Risk factors for late dislocation

Pseudoexfoliation syndrome

A major repeatedly reported association with progressive zonular weakness and late in-the-bag dislocation.

High myopia

Long axial length and associated ocular changes may coexist with increased zonular vulnerability.

Previous vitrectomy

Associated with increased risk of late instability and relevant to repeat-surgery planning because normal vitreous support is absent.

Trauma, uveitis, capsular contraction

Traumatic zonular damage, chronic inflammation and marked capsular phimosis can contribute to loss of support.

A risk factor alone does not dictate the secondary IOL. The operation is selected according to the actual anatomy: residual capsule and zonules, iris and endothelium, glaucoma status, vitreous and retinal findings, IOL design and surgeon experience.

04 · INITIAL ASSESSMENT

What to document at the first visit

  • Uncorrected and best-corrected visual acuity, refraction and symptoms: monocular diplopia, glare, arcs or unstable vision.
  • Intraocular pressure and evidence of secondary glaucoma.
  • Optic and haptic position in a small and dilated pupil, tilt and abnormal mobility of the IOL–bag complex with eye movement.
  • Anterior capsule, capsular bag and visible zonular support.
  • Corneal endothelium, anterior chamber, iris contact, inflammation or haemorrhage.
  • Anterior vitreous prolapse or vitreous incarceration at wounds.
  • Macula, optic nerve and retinal periphery when visualization allows.
05 · DIAGNOSTICS

Additional testing when it answers a management question

Anterior segment
  • Ultrasound biomicroscopy when haptics, sulcus or zonular support are poorly visualized.
  • Anterior-segment OCT as an adjunct in selected cases.
  • Specular microscopy when endothelial reserve affects urgency or IOL choice.
Posterior segment
  • Macular OCT for unexplained visual loss or suspected cystoid macular oedema.
  • B-scan ultrasonography when the fundus cannot be visualized.
  • Dilated peripheral retinal examination before surgery and for retinal symptoms.
06 · URGENCY

Situations requiring prompt assessment

!
Urgent ophthalmic review

IOL in the anterior chamber or endothelial contact; acute IOP elevation; marked pain and inflammation; recurrent haemorrhage from mechanical tissue contact; sudden major visual loss; or flashes, a new shower of floaters or field loss requiring exclusion of retinal tear/detachment.

Anti-inflammatory or IOP-lowering treatment alone is not definitive when continuing mechanical IOL contact or instability is maintaining iris injury, haemorrhage, inflammation or outflow obstruction.

07 · MAIN ALGORITHM

Observation or surgery?

1
Are symptoms functionally significant?

Reduced best-corrected vision, monocular diplopia, severe dysphotopsia or unstable vision favour intervention.

2
Is the IOL stable?

A small stable decentration may be observed. Progressive displacement or marked abnormal mobility favours surgery.

3
Are complications present?

IOP elevation, endothelial injury, iris trauma, inflammation, haemorrhage, macular oedema or retinal complications reduce the acceptability of observation.

4
How much capsular–zonular support remains?

This determines whether to preserve and secure the existing complex or remove/exchange the IOL.

08 · REPOSITION OR EXCHANGE

Keep the existing IOL or replace it?

QuestionFavours repositioningFavours IOL exchange
IOL design and integrityOptic and haptics intact; design suitable for the intended fixationDamage, unsuitable design or major optical defect
IOL–bag complexCan be reliably recentred and securedSevere fibrosis/deformation or unreliable centration
Surgical burdenPreservation reduces manipulation in that eyeAttempting to salvage the complex would be less safe or less durable

Randomized evidence in late in-the-bag dislocation indicates that both repositioning and IOL exchange can provide useful visual outcomes in appropriately selected patients, with different technical demands and complication profiles.

09 · SECONDARY FIXATION

Options when capsular support is inadequate

Sutured scleral fixation

Suitable IOLs may be secured to sclera; haptic design, suture longevity, knot management and symmetry are important.

Sutureless intrascleral fixation

Tunnel/flanged techniques are options for suitable three-piece IOLs in experienced hands; symmetric fixation and tilt control are essential.

Iris fixation

An option with adequate iris tissue and a suitable IOL, balanced against chronic inflammation, pupil distortion and mechanical contact.

Retropupillary iris-claw IOL

An aphakia solution when capsular support is absent; selection depends on iris, endothelium and surgeon experience.

Critical point: a single-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL designed for capsular-bag placement should not be treated as a routine sulcus-fixated lens because thick haptics/optic edge can cause iris chafing, pigment dispersion, inflammation and secondary glaucoma.
10 · VITRECTOMY

When a pars plana approach is useful

  • IOL or IOL–bag complex displaced posteriorly into the vitreous cavity or onto the retinal surface.
  • Vitreous prolapse prevents safe anterior manipulation or creates traction.
  • Posterior-segment pathology requires simultaneous treatment.
  • An anterior approach cannot provide safe control of the lens.

When vitreous has prolapsed anteriorly, define its extent, perform appropriate anterior or posterior vitrectomy, release traction and only then continue IOL repositioning or removal. Pulling vitreous together with the IOL transmits traction to the retinal periphery and should be avoided.

11 · SPECIAL SITUATIONS

Factors that modify the plan

Pseudoexfoliation syndrome

Expect zonular weakness to be more extensive than the visible defect; fixation should stabilize the whole complex for the long term.

Eye after vitrectomy

Loss of normal vitreous support and posterior-segment context call for controlled manipulation and readiness for a pars plana approach.

Glaucoma

Consider baseline IOP, optic-nerve damage, existing drainage/filtering surgery and the effect of the selected IOL on iris and angle.

Uveitis, trauma, high myopia

Control inflammation, define traumatic iris/capsular support, and assess the retinal periphery and biometric implications in long eyes.

12 · PREOPERATIVE CHECKLIST

Know the anatomy before starting

  • Original IOL model, material and power when records are available.
  • Position of both haptics and condition of the capsular bag.
  • Expected need for vitrectomy and presence of anterior vitreous prolapse.
  • Corneal endothelium, iris and angle status.
  • IOP and glaucoma status.
  • Macula and retinal periphery.
  • Plan A and backup plan B if intended fixation is not stable.
  • Biometry and new IOL calculation when exchange is planned.
13 · CRITICAL ERRORS

Errors that can worsen outcome

  • Recentering an IOL without defining the mechanism and extent of zonular failure.
  • Manipulating a dislocated IOL before vitreous traction is controlled.
  • Selecting a secondary IOL without considering iris, endothelium, angle and glaucoma.
  • Asymmetric fixation causing persistent tilt/decentration.
  • Treating inflammation or IOP alone when ongoing mechanical contact is the driver.
  • Failing to examine the retinal periphery after new flashes/floaters or significant vitreous traction.
14 · FOLLOW-UP

Monitoring should match the operation

Early reviews assess visual acuity, IOP, IOL centration and stability, anterior chamber reaction, cornea and wound status. Macular OCT is obtained for unexplained visual loss or cystoid macular oedema risk. The retinal periphery is reassessed when symptoms occur or after substantial vitreous manipulation.

15 · COMPLICATIONS

Targeted surveillance

Anterior segment
  • Recurrent IOL tilt/decentration.
  • Inflammation and iris trauma.
  • IOP elevation/secondary glaucoma.
  • Corneal endothelial decompensation.
  • Anterior chamber haemorrhage.
Posterior segment
  • Cystoid macular oedema.
  • Vitreous haemorrhage.
  • Retinal tears/detachment.
  • Endophthalmitis, a rare but serious intraocular-surgery complication.
16 · REFERRAL

Useful information when referring a patient

Best-corrected visual acuity, refraction when informative, intraocular pressure, position of the IOL and capsular bag, abnormal mobility of the complex, vitreous prolapse, macular and peripheral retinal status, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, previous vitrectomy, glaucoma and any urgent complication. Include original IOL model and power if known.

For colleagues

Refer a patient with IOL dislocation for consultation

For assessment of the dislocation mechanism, feasibility of preserving the existing IOL and selection of the most appropriate surgical correction.

17 · EVIDENCE

No single secondary-fixation method fits every eye

Comparative studies and systematic reviews support several approaches when patients are selected appropriately. Technique choice should therefore integrate ocular anatomy, IOL design, capsular/iris/endothelial status, glaucoma, posterior-segment findings, complication profile and surgeon experience.
